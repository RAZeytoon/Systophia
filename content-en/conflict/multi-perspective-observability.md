> [!question] What are we looking at here?
> If we look at a system from one angle only, what do we lose?

# Why no single observer is enough

We are used to seeing the world through one window, each of us with a pane of glass in hand, sure the picture is clear. But no pane is complete, no angle takes in the whole, and no observer, however sharp, sees all the hidden lines of a complex system. This is where "multi-perspective observability" makes sense: relying on one view, even the most exact engineering view or the most calculating economic view, leads to error. (I use this name to sum up this idea; it is not an established term.)

A complex system is like a walled garden. From outside you see only the high walls; maybe the smell of flowers drifts out, or a bird's song, but the plan inside is not yours. The more you walk around the wall, the more new corners you find, but the picture never completes. Systems, from a production line to a school or a city, have layers, signals and dependencies woven together. Look with one eye and you miss some of the patterns, the very ones that later turn into crisis or conflict.

# The orchestra

Picture an orchestra: violin, cello, trumpet and timpani each play their own instrument. Listen only to the violin and you understand the melody to a degree but lose the harmony of the whole. A system is the same:

- **The engineering view:** processes, mechanisms, bottlenecks and material flow
- **The human view:** motivation, fatigue, work culture and the psychological interaction of staff
- **The economic and social view:** cost-benefit logic, policies, power relations and outside pressures

Only when these instruments are heard together does the real piece, the collective behaviour of the system, show itself.

# Example: a beverage production line

Take a factory that makes fizzy drinks. The filling, packing and labelling machines work in precise coordination.

- **Engineering:** the maintenance engineer sees that the filler motor's temperature has risen and thinks it is a mechanical problem.
- **Human:** the HR team reports that night-shift workers have lost focus and manual skill because of constant overtime.
- **Economic:** finance shows that production volume doubled to meet advertising-driven demand, and short-term profit took priority.

With the engineering view alone we might decide to replace the motor. With the combined view we understand that the root lies in production policy, human pressure and organisational culture.

# Mutual dependencies

In a complex system no line dances alone. Every node, every decision and every action is tied to a network of dependencies. When the line stops, it is not only a mechanical problem; it may be the result of a management decision to win a new customer, or a response to a market shift and rising raw-material prices. Multi-perspective observability helps us see these hidden threads.

# From watching to designing

This view helps not only analysis but design. When you are about to build a new system or redesign one in crisis, the multi-perspective view makes you ask, before writing the first line of code or drawing the first diagram: which voices in this orchestra are still silent? Which relations are hidden under the skin of the system? Which data that looks unimportant now will turn into a crisis later?

# Beauty in seeing the whole

Systems are like Persian carpets: thousands of knots, each perhaps meaningless alone, together a picture that takes the heart. Multi-perspective observability invites us to gaze at this complex carpet, at the narratives, layers and warp and weft that quietly write a system's fate.
