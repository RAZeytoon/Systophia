# Lipstick on a Pig

## *Lipstick on a Pig: Winning in the No-Spin Era by Someone Who Knows the Game*

## 1. Book profile

- **Title:** *Lipstick on a Pig: Winning in the No-Spin Era by Someone Who Knows the Game*
- **Author:** Torie Clarke (full name Victoria Clarke)
- **Year of first publication:** 2006
- **Publisher of the original edition:** Free Press
- **Field:** strategic communication, public relations, political and corporate communication, and crisis management.
- **Kind of work:** a practical guide based on the author's professional experience.

The publisher's official page presents the book as a guide to the era of transparency and says that Clarke distils her 25 years of experience into eight rules of communication. She had earlier been Assistant Secretary of Defense for Public Affairs and a communications adviser to political and organisational figures. [Publisher's source](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Lipstick-on-a-Pig/Torie-Clarke/9780743271172)

## 2. Message and central thesis

The book's message can be summarised like this: in an environment with abundant information sources and fast-moving news, "spinning" the narrative or dressing up reality is a fragile strategy. Organisations should tell the truth early, repeatedly and in plain language; get ahead on bad news; admit mistakes quickly; and, instead of spending energy dressing up a bad decision, prevent it from being made in the first place.

The title comes from an English proverb: "you can put lipstick on a pig, but it's still a pig." The metaphor says that cosmetic dressing or a favourable narrative does not change the nature of a problem. If a product, decision or behaviour is genuinely flawed, positive communication alone does not fix it.

## 5. A few practical principles for organisations

1. **Check the reality before the message.** Communication must not take the place of fixing the decision or product.
2. **Start the message from the goal.** Positive coverage is not the final goal or a sufficient measure of success.
3. **Don't hide bad news.** Explain the verified facts, the impact, responsibility and the corrective action.
4. **Use plain language.** Complicated jargon should not be used to dodge the main question.
5. **Keep updating.** In a crisis, separate the knowns from the unknowns and report changes.
6. **Match the channel to the audience.** One tool or medium does not suit every audience.
7. **Measure the result.** Do not confuse the number of news items or views with understanding, trust or behaviour change.

## 6. Critique and limits of application

The book was published in 2006 and written in the media and communication context of that time. The principle of transparency is still important, but channels, the speed at which content circulates, social networks and fact-checking mechanisms have changed since; so its application today has to be revisited to suit the digital environment and audience expectations.

Risks of applying the book's principles wrongly:
- "Transparency" is read as disclosing personal or security data;
- "flooding the zone" turns into bombarding the audience with many messages or burying bad news;
- getting ahead is mistaken for hurrying to publish unverified information;
- an apology becomes a show, without practical correction;
- the success of communication is measured only by positive media coverage.
