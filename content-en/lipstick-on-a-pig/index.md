> [!question] What are we looking at here?
> What happens when something is broken at its foundation and we only touch up its surface?

"Lipstick on a pig" is an English idiom for cosmetic repair applied to something whose problem is structural. I think this one sentence holds a whole school of thought, because it turns up in fields far from each other. Here are four of them side by side:

**Bail-outs.** Simon Deane-Johns's 2012 book asks why public money for banks in crisis preserves the very structures that helped cause it. The bail-out is the lipstick; the structure is the pig. Start with [[lipstick-on-a-pig/financial-bailouts-deane-johns/index|the notes on the book]].

**Communication spin.** Torie Clarke's 2006 book uses the same metaphor for communication: if the product or decision is bad from the start, no packaging will save it, so do not build the pig in the first place. These two books share only the metaphor; they are different books on different subjects. See [[lipstick-on-a-pig/no-spin-era-clarke/index|the notes on the book]].

**Technology.** When we make a faulty process "smart", we only repeat its fault faster. I told this through a story about two toilets in [[design/automating-a-bad-smell|Automating a bad smell]].

**Architecture.** In *The Timeless Way of Building*, Christopher Alexander speaks of the "quality without a name": something surface decoration cannot produce, which comes out of a living process and living patterns. To me it is the same contrast from another angle: living structure versus a manufactured look. See [[lipstick-on-a-pig/timeless-way-of-building-alexander/index|the notes on the book]].

What the four share is that cosmetic repair **buys time and moves the cost elsewhere**, but does not solve the problem. The systems analyst's question is the same each time: *does this intervention actually change the structure, or only the appearance?*
