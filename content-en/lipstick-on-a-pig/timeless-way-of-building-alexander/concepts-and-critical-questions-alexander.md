# Concepts and Critical Questions

This note keeps the distinctions that must not be lost when retelling Christopher Alexander's theory.

## 1. Claim versus established fact

| Statement | Kind of statement | A more precise formulation |
|---|---|---|
| The quality without a name is objective and exact | Alexander's theoretical claim | "Alexander argues that…", followed by critical examination |
| Patterns solve a problem in a specific context | A design principle within the theory | The context and the problem must be made clear in each case |
| Modularity kills space | An over-generalisation | A critique of rigid standardisation and of ignoring local differences |
| A master plan destroys the order of a city | An over-generalisation | A critique of centralised control that ignores local feedback |
| Building should be done without drawings | A dangerous formulation | Feedback on site alongside drawings, calculations, regulations and safety |
| Traditional architecture is always alive | An unjustified historical generalisation | Quality must be judged by evidence and users' experience |

## 2. Key distinctions

- **Pattern / module:** a pattern is a relation that answers a problem; a module is a standardised unit.
- **Repetition / copy:** repeating a rule can produce context-sensitive variety; mechanical copying limits difference.
- **Timeless / historical:** "timeless" is a claimed, lasting quality, not necessarily a historical form.
- **Participation / removing control:** local participation matters, but safety and infrastructure may call for specialist coordination.
- **Egolessness / irresponsibility:** humility before reality is different from setting aside professional commitment.
- **Intuition / evidence:** a feeling of quality can be a clue; for general claims, an observation method and evidence are needed.
- **Event / space:** the experience of a place depends on both the physical arrangement and the patterns of events.

## 3. Analytical questions for each pattern

1. What exactly is the context?
2. Who are the real users and what different needs do they have?
3. Which forces conflict with each other?
4. What relationship does the pattern propose?
5. What evidence shows it is useful in this context?
6. What are the unintended consequences, or the groups left out?
7. What conditions call for changing or setting aside the pattern?
8. What dependencies exist with other patterns?
9. What should be reviewed during use and maintenance?

## 4. Limits of this set of notes

- The exact English terms against their Persian equivalents;
- the location of the discussion of "ageing and wear";
- whether "Invariant Core", "morphological completeness" and "functional completeness" are the book's own terms or the summariser's formulation;
- specific examples and evidence for each chapter;
- possible differences between the podcasts and the text of the book.
