> [!question] What are we looking at?
> Is the world a set of independent pieces or one unified whole? And where does the systems analyst stand in this argument?

# Two views of the world

Our view of the world and its phenomena comes from the kind of outlook we have. Do we take the world as a set of independent components, or as one unified whole whose parts only make sense in the context of their relations? These two views, **holism** and **atomism** (reductionism), play a foundational role not only in philosophy and science but also in systems analysis.

# Holism: from Aristotle to the Middle Ages

The roots of holism lie in old human thought. In the *Metaphysics* (Book VIII) Aristotle says that a whole is not a mere heap of parts; it is something besides the parts, and its unity has a cause. The popular line "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" is a modern paraphrase of that idea, not Aristotle's own sentence. Aristotle also didn't explain things by matter and mechanism alone; he counted the "final cause", a thing's purpose or function, as part of the explanation. The Middle Ages mostly kept that purposive, whole-first view: each thing had a place in a larger order and drew its meaning from that place, and to understand cosmic order or human phenomena people looked for the meaningful and moral relation between parts rather than the measurable, computational one.

# Reductionism: from Descartes and Newton to the age of scientism

Atomism itself is old (Democritus), but it became the *dominant method of science* in the seventeenth century. In the *Discourse on Method* (1637) Descartes makes it his second rule: divide each difficulty into as many parts as is feasible and necessary to solve it. In the same century the mathematical tools that made this powerful were built: Descartes' analytic geometry, calculus (Newton and Leibniz), and probability theory (the Pascal–Fermat correspondence of 1654). Newton's *Principia* (1687) then presented the world as a mechanical, measurable, lawful machine.

We know the result: the pace of science and technology rose, precision reached the nano scale, the power to predict and control phenomena grew, and specialisation spread; people became scientists or engineers in defined fields. The logic was simple: to know a phenomenon, it's enough to break it into smaller parts and examine each one separately. This logic led to the extraordinary development of technology and industry.

# But did everything go well?

At first glance it seemed modern man had conquered the world through scientism and reductionism. But critics like **Oswald Spengler** warned that technical growth doesn't necessarily lift a civilisation. In *The Decline of the West* he says civilisations, like living things, have birth, maturity and decline, and that Western civilisation has entered decline: cultural vitality has dropped, religious faith has weakened, and extreme materialism and rationalism have taken its place.

Later holists, from Spengler to the Club of Rome and thinkers in systems theory, repeat the same warning in another form: don't look only at the parts; understanding structure, relations and larger purposes matters more than knowing the parts alone.

# Where does systems analysis stand?

Both approaches are present in systems analysis:

|Approach|Focus|Strength|Weakness|
|---|---|---|---|
|Reductionism|The system's parts, separately|High precision, specialisation|Ignores interactions and larger goals|
|Holism|Relations between parts and the overall purpose|Understanding structure and coherence|Vagueness in measuring parts quantitatively|

I think a successful analyst is one who can **combine** the two: decompose first, then connect. Reductionism carried science and industry forward but sometimes ended in poverty of meaning and estrangement from the whole; holism is an old but deep view that rests on relations and purposes. Systems analysis is built on combining them: know the parts, but find their meaning in the structure of the whole.
