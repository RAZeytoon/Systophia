## When I do not know who I am dealing with

In a game of **incomplete information** a player does not know what "type" the opponent is (ability, cost, preference). **Harsanyi** (1967) offered an elegant solution: nature first chooses each player's "type" by a probability distribution, and each player sees only their own type. A game of incomplete information becomes a game of **imperfect information** and is solved with beliefs (probabilities).

## Bayesian equilibrium

In a **Bayesian Nash equilibrium** each type of each player, given their beliefs about the opponent's type, plays a best response. In dynamic games a **perfect Bayesian equilibrium** is also needed: beliefs are updated by Bayes' rule after seeing moves.

## Signaling

When the party with private information can **take an action that reveals their type**, **signaling** occurs. **Michael Spence** (1973) showed that education can be a signal: if education is cheaper for a capable worker, they show their ability by a degree and the market believes it, even if the degree adds nothing to productivity. Condition: **the cost of the signal must be higher for the weak** (otherwise everyone imitates).

Other examples: a warranty (a signal of quality), costly advertising, paying dividends.

## Empty threats and credibility

A threat whose execution hurts the threatener is **empty** and, if the other side is rational, has no effect. To have effect it must become **credible**: either by prior commitment (losing the option of retreat) or by **reputation** (in a repeated game). That is why those who are seen as "irrational" in bargaining sometimes win more.

## An example in negotiation

In [[04-bargaining-power-factors-and-cost-of-waiting|Bargaining: power factors and the cost of waiting]] we saw that asymmetric information creates delay: the side with a high value must show by **enduring delay** that it really has that value, and the other side must distinguish between "rejecting the offer" and "a signal".
