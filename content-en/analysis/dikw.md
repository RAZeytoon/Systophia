> [!quote] T. S. Eliot, "The Rock" (1934)
> Where is the wisdom we have lost in knowledge? Where is the knowledge we have lost in information?

# A pyramid that is everywhere, yet rarely looked at properly

You may not have heard the name **DIKW** (Data, Information, Knowledge, Wisdom), but you've certainly seen the picture: a four-level pyramid that takes data up to wisdom. It is one of the most widely used frameworks in information science and knowledge management, and, interestingly, its name is tied to a pioneer of **systems thinking**, not an IT specialist.

# Where did it come from?

Eliot wrote the lines above in 1934, which is the literary picture of this same idea. The formulation of the model is usually credited to **Russell Ackoff**, in his paper "From Data to Wisdom" (1989). It has earlier roots as well: Harlan Cleveland (1982) and Milan Zeleny (1987) had written similar pyramids. Ackoff was a theorist of operations research and an important figure in systems thinking, and I think that matters: this model was made to understand organisations and systems, not just databases.

# Four levels (and one that usually gets dropped)

Ackoff knew each level by a question:

|Level|What it means|Organisational example|
|---|---|---|
|**Data**|Raw, uninterpreted symbols|"120"|
|**Information**|Data in context; answers who, what, where, how many|"120 orders were placed in July 2026"|
|**Knowledge**|Knowing "how"; a repeating pattern|"Orders peak every Thursday"|
|**Understanding**|Grasping "why"; the reason for that pattern|"Because salaries are paid at the end of the week"|
|**Wisdom**|Evaluative judgement; what is best to do|"Add warehouse staff for that day, but consider their fatigue too"|

The common four-level "DIKW" version has dropped the understanding level; Ackoff's own paper has five concepts.

# Critiques that shouldn't be ignored

The pyramid is so simple and appealing that the risk of believing it blindly is high. Some serious critiques apply:

- **It isn't linear.** The path from data to wisdom isn't a one-way staircase; often earlier knowledge or understanding decides what data is gathered in the first place (which connects to the piece on theory-first or data-first).
- **The boundaries aren't clear.** The line between information and knowledge isn't always clear in practice.
- **Wisdom can't be operationalised.** Data and information can be measured, wisdom can't; so many "turn data into insight" projects stall at the information and knowledge level while claiming insight.

# What does it matter to a systems analyst?

Most failures of data-driven projects (including enterprise-system roll-outs) come from here: the organisation thinks that because it has data and reports it has insight. But having a report is far from understanding "why" and deciding well. This is the same point we made in "Automating a bad smell": data and automated reports, if the process and decision-making behind them are faulty, don't become insight.
